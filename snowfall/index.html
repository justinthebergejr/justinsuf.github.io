NEW ENGLAND · 1940–2019 · NOAA GHCN-DAILY

Was there more
snow back then?

Born in 1939, my uncle remembered snowier days. We put his memory to the test: six states, eighty years, and the weather stations that were there for both.

No — not less snowfall in this comparison.

The published 21-station index shows 9.0% more annual snowfall in 2000–2019 than in 1940–1999. Uncertainty intervals include zero; this is not proof of a persistent increase.

01 / THE COMPARISON

Not one storm. Two eras.

We compare mean annual snowfall in 1940–1999 with 2000–2019. “Snow” means new snow falling, not the depth or duration of snow on the ground. New England means Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, and Vermont.

All versions require at least 40 accepted years in the earlier period and 14 in the later one (at least two thirds of each era). Each sensitivity rebuilds its fixed station panel; it tests both missingness rules and the resulting station selection. If a panel loses any state, no six-state result is reported.

04 / METHODS & LIMITS

An answer you can audit.

  1. Select without looking at the change. Start with every station in the six states whose GHCN inventory lists SNOW beginning by 1960 and ending in or after 2013. These are necessary endpoints for obtaining 40 earlier and 14 later years, not evidence of completeness.
  2. Read the original daily records. Parse NOAA’s fixed-width .dly files. SNOW is in millimeters; divide by 25.4 for inches. Reject missing values (-9999), negative values and every nonblank quality flag. Trace snowfall contributes zero measurable millimeters. NOAA’s “presumed zero” values are retained as supplied.
  3. Do not turn missing days into zero-snow days. Accept a calendar year only when every month has at least 95% valid daily readings. Sum accepted observations without filling or scaling. A small number of unobserved days can still understate totals. Keep only stations with at least two thirds of years accepted in each era (40/60 and 14/20). The same stations serve both periods; their individual accepted years can differ. An initial requirement of a pre-1941 start and 80% of years left some states unrepresented; these coverage rules were revised before calculating a regional result.
  4. Compare like with like. For each station, compute 100 × (later mean / earlier mean − 1). Average those percentages within each state, then average all six states. This avoids letting snowier sites dominate, or letting states with more stations dominate. It describes a typical state's sampled relative change—not regional total inches, population exposure, or a land-area average.
  5. Test variability, not just direction. Use 5,000 bootstrap replicates, resampling 60 earlier and 20 later years separately. All stations share each year draw to retain spatial dependence; absent station-years are omitted. Repeat with circular five-year blocks to allow short-range temporal dependence. Percentile intervals are conditional on this station panel, not a correction for measurement bias.

What this cannot tell us

Snowfall observations are not homogenized here. Station moves, observer practices, measurement changes, and surviving-station bias can affect the comparison. Equal state weighting is a transparent choice, not the only definition of “New England.” The long-record panel may miss mountain terrain or particular neighborhoods. Calendar years split winters, but match the decades in the question exactly.

A snowier total can coexist with faster melting, fewer snow-covered days, or a shorter winter. We did not analyze snow depth (SNWD) or attribute changes to climate forcing. Nor does a two-period comparison establish a monotonic trend. My uncle’s address and precise meaning of “less snow” are unknown: the verdict applies to the stated regional snowfall test, not to the truth of his personal experience.
